# When AI Does the Grunt Work, Who Trains the Next Expert?

Every profession used to run on the same quiet deal: juniors do the boring, low-stakes work — the document review, the first-draft model, the routine code, the basic research — and in exchange for tolerating the tedium, they build the judgment that makes them senior one day. The grunt work was never really about the output. It was the training ground.

Agentic AI is very good at exactly that grunt work. Which means the training ground is disappearing faster than anyone is replacing it.

---

## 1. This isn't new — but it's happening everywhere at once

The pattern has a name in the automation literature: **deskilling**. Concern that machines strip a job of the very content that used to build a worker's skill goes back to the earliest days of computing (Mann and Williams, 1960s). What's new is the scope. Generative AI is a general-purpose technology, so it isn't automating one task in one industry — it's automating the entry-level cognitive work of law, consulting, finance, medicine, and software all at once.

But deskilling is not automatic or inevitable. A 2026 review by Crowston and Bolici ([*"Deskilling and upskilling with generative AI systems"*](https://crowston.syr.edu/sites/default/files/GAI_and_skills.pdf)) modeled dozens of field studies and found the outcome depends entirely on how the task is divided between human and AI. When the AI hands over a finished answer and the human's role shrinks to "accept or reject," skill erodes. When the human still has to prompt from scratch, evaluate the output critically, and substantially edit it, skill holds — or even improves. In one study of AI-assisted sales calls, the AI took over the tedious lead-qualification step and left humans with the harder, judgment-heavy close — and the humans got *better* at it. The tool is not the deciding factor. The task design is.

This maps onto something learning science has known for decades. Lave and Wenger's concept of **legitimate peripheral participation** describes how novices become experts: by doing low-stakes, peripheral tasks inside a community of practice and gradually moving toward full participation. The grunt work was never a distraction from learning — it *was* the on-ramp. And Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper "Ironies of Automation" predicted the exact shape of today's problem: when automation absorbs the routine part of a task, the human is left either idle or stuck with only the hardest edge cases, without ever having built, through repetition, the skill needed to handle them.

---

## 2. Why this won't fix itself

It would be reassuring to think good training programs solve this. They help, but they don't solve the structural problem underneath.

Economist Miao Ben Zhang's 2026 paper ([*"The Broken Job Ladder: AI, Vanishing Apprenticeships, and the Role of Universities"*](https://www.miaobenzhang.com/BrokenLadder.pdf)) explains why. For decades, firms could pay juniors less than their marginal output in exchange for on-the-job learning — judgment developed as a *free byproduct* of production. AI breaks that bundle: once routine cognitive tasks are cheaper to automate than to assign to a junior, firms rationally stop assigning them — and stop developing juniors at all.

The result is a market failure, not a training gap. Any single firm that invests in developing a junior risks losing that now-trained senior to a competitor who paid nothing for the training. Combine that poaching risk with managers whose incentives are measured in quarters, not decades, and the equilibrium tips toward under-investment — in the worst case, toward what industry commentary has started calling the **"barbell organization"**: AI-heavy and senior-heavy, with a hollowed-out middle and no pipeline feeding the top.

---

## 3. What organizations can actually do

None of this means the apprenticeship model has to die — it means it has to be redesigned on purpose, because it will no longer happen by accident.

**Triage tasks before you automate them.** Not all grunt work is equal. Some of it teaches nothing; some of it is where judgment is actually built. Sort tasks into automate (no learning value), augment (AI drafts, human owns the judgment call), and preserve (kept manual specifically because the struggle is the point) — before defaulting to automating everything that can be automated.

**Require the struggle before the assist.** Shell's redesigned junior workflow is a good template: juniors must independently frame the problem and produce a baseline analysis *before* they're allowed to bring in AI to refine it. This isn't nostalgia — it's the learning-science principle of "desirable difficulty": the struggle is what encodes the skill, not the sight of a correct answer.

**Make "supervising the AI" a taught, graded skill — not an assumption.** If mandatory, substantive review of AI output preserves skill (per the Crowston/Bolici findings), then organizations need to actually teach and evaluate that review, not assume it happens by osmosis. Catching a subtly wrong AI output is a competency with its own learning curve.

**Manufacture the visibility that used to be free.** Juniors used to learn by watching seniors think out loud in shared rooms. AI-mediated work happens silently on individual screens — the incidental observation is gone. Paired sessions, "show your reasoning" reviews, and scheduled coaching have to replace what used to be ambient.

**Measure judgment development, not just throughput.** If managers are only scored on speed and cost, they will keep optimizing juniors out of the loop — that's the managerial myopia Zhang's model identifies as a root cause. Coaching juniors needs to be a funded, evaluated part of a manager's job, not unpaid overhead.

**Recognize when the fix has to be bigger than one company.** Because training investment can be poached away, internal-only fixes tend to be chronically underfunded. Zhang's proposed remedy — university- or consortium-designed "micro-residencies" that bundle a real deliverable with mandatory judgment exercise, on the model of medical residency or clinical legal education — points at the right scale of solution: something that doesn't collapse the moment one firm tries to free-ride on another's training spend.

---

## In one sentence

AI doesn't have to end the apprenticeship model, but it will, by default, unless organizations deliberately redesign which struggles they let juniors keep — because the training ground that used to build itself for free now has to be built on purpose.
