# We Automated the Grunt Work. We Forgot It Was Also the Classroom.

Every profession has run on the same quiet deal: juniors do the boring, low-stakes work, and in exchange they build the judgment that makes them senior one day. AI agents are now very good at exactly that boring work — which means we're quietly dismantling the training ground without meaning to.

→ Hand AI a finished task and only ask a junior to accept or reject it, and their skill erodes.
→ Make them attempt it first, then use AI to check their work, and their skill holds — or even grows.

Same technology. Opposite outcome. The difference is design.

Training a junior now costs real money that AI could save you. That tradeoff — automation now versus capability later — needs to be a deliberate part of how you design the workflow, not an afterthought.

Here's what's actually working for organizations taking this seriously:

1. Sort the work. Not all "grunt work" is equal — some of it teaches nothing, some of it is where judgment gets built. Automate the first. Protect the second.

2. Struggle before assist. Make juniors take a first crack at the problem before AI helps. The struggle is what builds the skill — not seeing the right answer.

3. Teach AI oversight as its own skill. Spotting a subtly wrong AI answer is a real competency. Don't assume people pick it up on their own — teach it and test it.

4. Make mentorship visible again. Juniors used to learn by watching seniors think out loud. AI work happens silently on a screen. Bring that thinking back into the open — walk-throughs, paired sessions, real coaching time.

5. Measure judgment, not just speed. If managers are only scored on output, they'll keep cutting juniors out of the loop. Coaching has to be a real, counted part of the job.

The apprenticeship model doesn't have to die. But it won't survive by accident anymore — it has to be built on purpose.
