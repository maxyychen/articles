## 1. Global Context and Demographic Pressures

The aging population presents significant socioeconomic challenges worldwide. Projections indicate continued growth in the elderly demographic, prompting governments to implement various funding mechanisms and policy reforms. In the United States, the healthcare system faces a supply crisis unable to meet the rising and complex needs of the aging population, suffering from labor shortages and resource constraints affecting efficiency, equity, and quality of care.

**Key statistics:**
- Between 2024-2034, the direct care workforce is projected to add over 772,000 new jobs—the largest growth of any job sector
- During the same period, 9.7 million total direct care jobs will need to be filled
- In Australia, 243,000 individuals live in approximately 2,700 residential aged care facilities yearly

## 2. Quality of Care Challenges

### 2.1 Key Quality Themes

A systematic review identified **nine key factors** influencing quality care from residents' perspectives:

1. Staffing levels
2. Staff attitudes
3. Continuity of care
4. Routine and predictability
5. Physical environment
6. Decision-making and choice
7. Dignity of risk
8. Meaningful activities
9. Culture and spirituality

### 2.2 Systemic Failures

A 2024 scoping study identified 38 articles addressing systemic failures across four major contexts: United States (14 studies), Canada (2), Australia (11), and European countries (11). Common issues include:

- Care quality concerns
- Safety issues
- Inability to meet consumer expectations
- Disconnect between policy objectives and service delivery

### 2.3 Health-Related Quality of Life

A comprehensive review analyzing 72 studies identified enduring challenges in:

- Physical and medical issues
- Psychological difficulties
- Socioeconomic obstacles
- Policy-related barriers

### 2.4 Missed Care

Twenty-four publications across 11 regions examined missed care measurement, with two principal methods identified: modified standard scales and tailored specific approaches. These were applied inconsistently and generated diverse measurement outcomes.

## 3. Workforce Crisis

### 3.1 Retention Challenges

Retention of care support workers remains a **global challenge** with:

- Rapid turnover rates
- Low job satisfaction
- Poorly defined career pathways

**Turnover statistics (2019-2020 data):**

| Role | Turnover Rate |
|------|--------------|
| Registered Nurses | 37% |
| Enrolled Nurses | 28% |
| Personal Care Workers | 28% |
| Allied Health Professionals | 25% |
| Allied Health Assistants | 28% |

The median annual turnover for nursing assistants in nursing homes was approximately **94-100%** in 2017-2018, with mean turnover rates exceeding 129% for CNAs. Turnover in home care was nearly **75%** in 2024.

### 3.2 Retention Factors

Two broad themes emerged from systematic reviews:

1. **Individual factors**: Personal satisfaction, positive relationships with staff/families/residents, cooperative workplace culture
2. **Organizational factors**: Management support, career development opportunities, adequate resourcing

### 3.3 Worker Wellbeing

A 2025 systematic review (52 studies from 1,121 aged care facilities involving 16,372 caregivers) found the sector faces significant challenges from:

- Rising demand from aging populations
- Chronic disease burden
- Workforce shortages contributing to staff stress, burnout, and poor mental wellbeing
- Median wage of just $17.36/hour (2024), with 36% living in or near poverty

## 5. Person-Centered Care (PCC)

### 5.1 Core Elements

PCC interventions are typically multifactorial, comprising:

- Environmental enhancement
- Social stimulation opportunities
- Leadership and management changes
- Staff empowerment models
- Consistent caregiver assignment
- Individualized philosophy of care

### 5.2 Resident Perspectives

A meta-synthesis revealed three themes from residents' viewpoints:

1. "Promotion of mutual understanding through communication"
2. "Care that acknowledges the independence of residents"
3. "Finding the optimized state"

PCC from residents' perspective is expressed as **"nursing that respects residents' independent existence."**

### 5.3 Implementation Enablers

Four interrelated themes for successful PCC implementation (from 63 original articles):

1. Knowing and confirming the patient as a whole person
2. Co-creating tailored personal health plans
3. Inter-professional teamwork and collaboration
4. Building a person-centered foundation

### 5.4 Key Finding

Notably, neither the amount of training nor financial resources dedicated to PCC were associated with implementation effectiveness. Instead, success depends on:

- High-quality management communications about change
- Organizational readiness for change
- Favorable perceptions from direct care providers

### 5.5 Culture Change Movement

The Pioneer Network, Eden Alternative, and Green House Project are leaders in the culture change movement that encourages models of care meeting a person's holistic needs via care practices and the physical environment.

## 6. Dementia Care

### 6.1 Prevalence and Impact

The 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia provides new evidence about dementia prevention, intervention, and care. As people live longer, the number of people who live with dementia continues to rise.

### 6.2 Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Meta-analyses revealed that non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) are effective for managing behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD):

**Effective interventions include:**
- Reminiscence therapy
- Massage therapy
- Music therapy
- Exercise therapy
- Combined massage and touch therapy
- Cognitive stimulation plus exercise plus social interaction
- Behavioural activation
- Acceptance and commitment therapy
- Cognitive behavioural therapy

### 6.3 Implementation Barriers

A 2025 systematic review identified barriers to implementing non-pharmacological therapies:

- Financial outlays associated with program delivery
- Infrastructure adaptation requirements
- Intervention-specific materials costs
- Limited staff training

**Key enabler:** Staff equipped with structured, intervention-specific training demonstrated higher acceptability and engagement.

### 6.4 Staff Training Innovation

Virtual reality technology has emerged as an innovative tool for dementia care training. A scoping review of nine articles (2015-2024) involving 362 formal caregivers found potential improvements in training outcomes.

### 6.5 Occupational Stress in Dementia Caregivers

A 2025 framework synthesis of 52 studies from 1,121 aged care facilities found that mitigating occupational stress for professional dementia caregivers is critical to workforce sustainability, care quality, health, and economic burden.

## 7. Palliative and End-of-Life Care

### 7.1 The Need for Palliative Care

- A significant proportion of Americans spend time in nursing homes at the end of life (historical estimates suggested up to one-third, though more recent data indicates approximately 10-20% die in nursing homes)
- People living in care homes are highly likely to die there, making these places where palliative care is needed
- U.S. nursing home expenditures totaled approximately $196.8 billion in 2020, with costs highest in the last months of life

### 7.2 Current Evidence

A systematic review examining 13 unique clinical trials found:

- In six trials, interventions enhanced nursing home palliative care processes
- Increased use of care pathways
- Higher hospice referrals
- Better documentation of pain and symptom assessment

**However**, in most trials there was mixed or insufficient evidence about the effect of palliative care interventions on:
- Reducing potentially burdensome care
- Resident/surrogate symptom ratings
- Advance care planning discussions and documentation
- Family members' evaluation of care

### 7.3 Unmet Needs

Many individuals receiving post-acute and long-term care services have unmet palliative and end-of-life care needs. There is substantial evidence that palliative and end-of-life care in nursing homes is often inadequate.

### 7.4 Key Recommendations

1. Promote internal palliative and end-of-life care capacity through comprehensive training and support
2. Ensure payment policies and regulations do not create barriers to delivering high-quality care
3. Align nursing home quality measures to include palliative and end-of-life care-sensitive indicators
4. Support access to and integration of external palliative care services
5. Improve end-of-life care programs in spirituality, advance directive planning, and family involvement

## 8. Falls Prevention

### 8.1 Prevalence and Impact

Approximately **25–35%** of older adults (≥65 years) experience one or more falls each year (CDC reports 1 in 4; WHO estimates 28-35%). This percentage increases to around 40% in individuals aged 85 and above. Falls are associated with substantial burden including:

- Healthcare system costs
- Individual injuries and fractures
- Reduced functioning and quality of life

### 8.2 Evidence for Exercise Interventions

A systematic review published in *Age and Ageing* (December 2023) found:

- **Strong evidence** that exercise reduces falls in community-dwelling older people
- **Moderate certainty evidence** that exercise is recommended for fall prevention in aged care residents who are willing and able to participate
- **High certainty evidence** that exercise has little or no lasting effect after the programme ends

**Key implication:** Exercise should be incorporated into routine ongoing care.

### 8.3 Comparison Between Settings

An umbrella review in *BMC Geriatrics* (2024) found:

| Intervention | Community Setting | Residential Aged Care |
|-------------|------------------|----------------------|
| Exercise | Consistent improvements | Consistent improvements |
| Vitamin D | Consistent improvements | No difference |
| Multifactorial | Variable | More consistent outcomes |

### 8.4 Implementation Factors

Interventions that are **simple, engaging, tailored, and supported by leadership** were more effectively adopted.

### 8.5 Evidence Summary

Twenty-six pieces of evidence were summarized across 8 areas:
1. Fall risk screening
2. Fall risk assessment
3. Exercise interventions
4. Medication management
5. Environmental safety
6. Health education
7. Psychological interventions
8. Multifactorial interventions

## 9. Medication Management and Polypharmacy

### 9.1 Prevalence

- **60-90%** of nursing home residents receive five or more medications
- Up to **70%** may receive more than nine medications daily

### 9.2 Risks and Consequences

Polypharmacy in advancing age frequently results in:

- Drug therapy problems related to interactions
- Drug toxicity
- Falls with injury
- Delirium
- Nonadherence
- Increased hospitalizations
- Emergency department visits
- Mobility issues

**Risk factors for adverse drug events:**
- Being a new resident
- Taking 9+ regularly scheduled medications
- Taking antibiotics, antipsychotics, or antidepressants

### 9.3 Management Strategies

**Primary strategies:**
1. Regular medication reviews in light of clinical status and care goals
2. Comprehensive medication reviews
3. Deprescribing unnecessary medications
4. Using tools such as the Beers Criteria to identify potentially inappropriate medications

### 9.4 Evidence from Interventions

| Study Type | Finding |
|-----------|---------|
| Geriatrician-led intervention (160 patients) | Significant decrease in total medications (16.64 to 15.54), high-risk medications, and drug-drug interactions |
| Multidisciplinary team approach (426 residents) | 39% elimination of inappropriate medications vs 30% in control |

### 9.5 Barriers

- Gaps in medication reconciliation
- Limited pharmacist engagement (especially in rural areas)
- Communication challenges between consultants, prescribers, and nursing staff
- Clinicians finding deprescribing complex despite feeling comfortable with it

## 10. Mental Health in Aged Care

### 10.1 Prevalence

- **65-90%** of nursing home residents have a mental disorder
- Depression affects approximately **30-50%** of nursing home residents
- Anxiety disorders are common in older adults living in nursing homes

### 10.2 Comparison with Community-Dwelling Elderly

Compared with elderly people living in their own homes, nursing home residents reported:
- Lower self-esteem
- Higher depression
- Higher anxiety

### 10.3 Contributing Factors

- **Relocation stress**: The move from home to residential care is one of the most stressful life experiences
- Loss of loved ones
- Social isolation
- Declining physical health
- Chronic pain (2.5–4.1 times more likely to be depressed)
- Cognitive impairment

### 10.4 Gaps in Care

- **80%** of nursing home residents with mental illness did not receive mental health visits from a psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, or licensed clinical social worker
- Most nursing homes lack access to mental health providers with psychiatric training
- Nursing assistants are generally not well-equipped to identify depression
- Symptoms often go untreated due to coexisting health needs

### 10.5 Treatment Approaches

- Non-pharmacological treatments for depression
- Early identification and intervention
- Staff training in mental health recognition
- Positive social interactions
- Tailored recreational programs

## 11. Nutrition and Malnutrition

### 11.1 Prevalence

| Setting | Malnutrition Prevalence |
|---------|------------------------|
| Community (non-institutionalized) | 1-15% |
| Residential aged care | 43.1% moderately malnourished, 6.4% severely malnourished |
| Geriatric care facilities | 25-60% |
| Hospitals | 35-65% |
| Australian community | 1-8% malnourished |
| Australian aged care | Up to 68% at risk or malnourished |

### 11.2 Risk Factors

Significant risk factors include:
- Age and frailty
- Excessive polypharmacy
- Physical function decline
- Parkinson disease
- Constipation
- Poor self-reported health status
- Cognitive decline and dementia
- Eating dependencies
- Loss of interest in life
- Poor appetite
- Oral dysphagia
- Signs of impaired swallowing
- Institutionalization

### 11.3 Consequences

Malnutrition is associated with:
- Increased risk of infections
- Falls
- Pressure ulcers
- Hospital admissions
- Increased healthcare costs
- Poorer quality of life

### 11.4 Evidence on Interventions

Meta-analyses revealed a **significant positive relationship** between nutritional status and quality of life, and that nutritional intervention significantly improved QoL.

### 11.5 Clinical Recommendations

- Routine screening for malnutrition in all older adults
- Nutritional assessment for positive screening tests
- Individually tailored nutritional support including:
  - Nutritional advice and counselling
  - Oral nutritional supplements
  - Fortified foods
  - Enteral or parenteral nutrition as required

### 11.6 Challenges

In clinical practice, incorporation of nutritional guidelines is inadequate and low-value care is commonplace. Malnutrition and frailty are preventable and reversible.

## 12. Infection Prevention and Control

### 12.1 Burden of Healthcare-Associated Infections

- Point prevalence studies show **2.0–4.4%** of nursing home residents are affected by healthcare-associated infections (HAI)
- Residential aged care facilities frequently experience outbreaks of viral respiratory tract infections and gastroenteritis

### 12.2 Vulnerability Factors

- Close proximity living
- Shared living areas and bathrooms
- Frequent close interactions with staff and visitors
- Wandering behaviours in some residents
- Advanced age and frailty
- Multiple comorbidities

### 12.3 Effective Interventions

A systematic literature review found:
- **Vaccination** was highly effective in reducing infections in outbreak settings
- Strong support for widespread surveillance
- Early identification and response
- Rigorous infection prevention and control measures

### 12.4 CDC Recommendations

Basic infection prevention and control guidelines include:
1. Disinfection and sterilization
2. Environmental infection control (air, water, surfaces, laundry)
3. Proper medical waste handling
4. Hand hygiene best practices
5. Isolation precautions with education and training

### 12.5 Implementation Challenges

- Training of professionals does not reach hospital-level standards
- Difficulty establishing infection containment measures
- Need for dedicated IPC lead in every facility (now mandated in Australia)

## 13. COVID-19 Pandemic: Impact and Lessons Learned

### 13.1 Devastating Impact

- Nursing home residents constituted **one-third to more than half** of all deaths during early pandemic waves
- **2 in 5** Medicare beneficiaries in nursing homes had or likely had COVID-19 in 2020
- More than 1,300 nursing homes had infection rates of **75% or higher** during surge periods
- Care homes accounted for **40-80%** of COVID-19 deaths in the first six months

### 13.2 Contributing Factors

- High occupancy density
- Shared living areas
- Residents with cognitive and behavioural issues
- Lack of human and material resources
- Antiquated infrastructure
- Community transmission levels
- Nursing home size and quality
- For-profit status
- Sociodemographic characteristics

### 13.3 Staffing Challenges

Nursing homes faced:
- Significant loss of staff
- Substantial difficulties in hiring, training, and retaining new staff
- Reliance on outside staffing agencies (with significant downsides)

### 13.4 Impact of Protective Measures

Protective measures (absence of social gatherings, visitor restrictions, activity limitations, ban on group-eating) induced long periods of social isolation, requiring delicate balance against their impact on resident, family, and staff well-being.

### 13.5 Key Lessons and Recommendations

**HHS Office of Inspector General recommendations:**
1. CMS should implement policies to strengthen the nursing home workforce
2. CMS should update nursing home infection control requirements
3. CMS should provide effective guidance and assistance on compliance

**Future priorities:**
1. Recruitment and retention of well-trained, geriatric medicine–attuned workforce
2. Adequate investment, financing, and regulation
3. Coordination of complex aspects of care within nursing homes
4. Optimal chronic disease and scheduled routine medical care
5. Specialist input from geriatric medicine, psychiatry, and palliative medicine
6. Review of architectural design principles

## 14. Family Caregiver Involvement

### 14.1 Impact on Outcomes

- Residents who received visitors were more likely to discharge from a nursing home alive
- Caregivers providing >35 hours of informal care per week were most likely to discharge their loved ones
- Informal care may positively affect care quality in long-term care environments

### 14.2 Types of Family Involvement

A scoping review identified four themes:

1. **Contact**: Visitation, calling, writing letters
2. **Engagement in care activities**: Instrumental/activities of daily living
3. **Planning and monitoring care**: Awareness of health changes, partnership with staff, ensuring adequate care, decision-making
4. **Supporting the resident**: Advocacy, socioemotional support, financial support

### 14.3 Benefits

Family caregiver contributions have been shown to:
- Reduce risk of hospitalisation
- Promote participation in leisure activities
- Preserve resident quality of life and well-being

### 14.4 Best Practices

- Interventions focused on **cocreation**
- Good partnership between family and staff
- Staff supporting family caregivers
- Family-centred approach focusing on caregivers' personal needs
- Family caregivers seen as equal partners with active roles

### 14.5 Policy Developments

- As of 2022, **45 states and territories** have enacted the CARE Act (Caregiver Advise, Record, and Enable)
- Tennessee has established value-based payment models that consider family engagement

### 14.6 Ongoing Caregiver Roles

More than half of caregivers:
- Monitor care recipient health status
- Manage care
- Assist with meals

40% assist with self-care tasks.

## 15. Quality Indicators and Measurement

### 15.1 Commonly Used Instruments

**Generic preference-based instruments:**
- EuroQol-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) - most commonly applied
- Adult Social Care Outcomes Toolkit (ASCOT)
- Health Utilities Index (HUI2/3)

**Older person specific instruments:**
- ICEpop CAPability measure for Older people (ICECAP-O) - most widely applied

### 15.2 Quality Indicator Distribution

Of 45 unique quality indicators identified:
- **93%** were outcome measures
- **7%** measured care processes
- **0%** measured structure of care

### 15.3 Australia's National Quality Indicator Programme

**Initial indicators (July 2019–June 2021):**
1. Pressure injuries
2. Unintended weight loss
3. Use of physical restraints

**Added indicators (July 2021):**
4. Falls
5. Medication management

### 15.4 End-of-Life Care Quality Indicators

From seven studies, 106 EOLC quality indicators (75 unique) were identified:
- 31 focused on structure and process of care
- 51 identified physical and psychological aspects of care

### 15.5 Methodological Challenges

- Considerable variability in methodological quality between indicator sets
- Only 35% had high methodological quality on at least two of three domains
- Risk adjustment is particularly important for outcome measures
- No single instrument stood out as best suited for all aged care settings

### 16.2 Five Roles of AI in Elderly Care

1. Rehabilitation therapists
2. Emotional supporters

- Smart homes for elderly
- AI-based health monitoring technologies
- Wearable devices
- Gamification technologies

- Potential for continuous monitoring in real-time
- May reduce hospital admissions through virtual monitoring

- Publications increased sharply from 2014 to 2022 (90.87% of all publications)
- "Machine learning" had the highest burst strength among keywords

- Risk of exacerbating health inequity
- Privacy and autonomy concerns

- Workflow integration challenges
- Cost barriers
- Resistance to change
- Need for ethical guidelines

## 17. Access and Equity Issues

### 17.1 Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD) Communities

A 2024 scoping review highlighted barriers facing CALD older adults in accessing aged care services, emphasizing the need for culturally appropriate awareness in policies and interventions.

### 17.2 First Nations Populations

## 18. Policy and Reform Landscape

### 18.1 Shift Toward Consumer-Driven Approaches

The fundamental change from provider-focused to consumer-driven approaches has resulted in:

- Uncertainty and instability for residential aged care providers
- Challenges to financial viability (especially for small, single-site, non-profit providers)
- Disconnect between government policy and consumer expectations

### 18.2 Home Care Preference

Evidence shows:

- Most people prefer to receive services at home
- Many OECD countries have implemented plans supporting home care
- Ratio of long-term care recipients (65+) receiving home care has increased over the last decade
- Case management improves function, medication appropriateness, community service use, and reduces nursing home admissions

### 18.3 Accreditation Challenges

While accreditation remains a key quality improvement activity, the large number of low-frequency issues illustrates the complex nature of caring for aged care residents. Targeting each individual issue requires significant resources and accessing evidence for what works can be challenging, particularly for smaller organizations.

### 18.4 Recent Policy Developments

Australia recently passed the **Aged Care Bill 2024**, reflecting a shift towards a rights-based, person-centred approach.
