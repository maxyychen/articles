# LLMs in oncology clinical decision support: promise meets reality

**The transformational potential of large language models in cancer care remains largely unrealized as of 2025.** Despite remarkable technical advances and hundreds of validation studies, not a single LLM-based Clinical Decision Support System has received FDA approval for treatment recommendations in oncology, and no randomized controlled trials demonstrate improved patient outcomes. The field stands at a critical inflection point: sophisticated architectures exist and validation studies show 60-95% concordance with expert tumor boards, yet clinical deployment remains confined to research settings, administrative tasks, and heavily supervised pilot programs. This gap between technological capability and clinical reality stems from persistent safety concerns (hallucination rates of 1.5-23% with 44% classified as major errors), unresolved liability questions, algorithmic bias that threatens to exacerbate existing 30% mortality disparities, and regulatory frameworks still catching up to rapidly evolving AI capabilities. Leading cancer centers including Memorial Sloan Kettering, Dana-Farber, and Stanford have developed sophisticated governance models and specialized oncology LLMs, while oncologists express cautious optimism (52-95% see potential benefits) tempered by substantial concerns about explainability, bias detection, and clinical autonomy.

## The deployment reality check across major cancer centers

Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center has established the most sophisticated AI governance framework identified in this research, the **iLEAP (Legal, Ethics, Adoption, Performance) lifecycle management model**. As of 2025, MSK monitors 26 registered AI models including LLMs through rigorous gate reviews (G1-G5 stages), with only 2 of 19 production models meeting Express Path criteria for accelerated deployment. The institution developed **Woollie**, an oncology-specific LLM achieving **0.97 AUROC** for cancer progression prediction on internal data and maintaining **0.88 AUROC** on external validation at UCSF—the first open-source oncology LLM validated across institutions. However, MSK's implementation requires intensive resources: 11 core staff plus 10 offshore consultants dedicated to AI/ML support, with AI project intake volume increasing 63% from 2024 to 2025.

Dana-Farber Cancer Institute deployed GPT4DFCI, a private HIPAA-compliant GPT-4 instance reaching 2,000+ users across the institution, but **explicitly prohibits its use for direct clinical care**. The system serves administrative, research, and operational functions only, with users reminded they remain "directly responsible for completeness, veracity, and fairness" of all outputs. Dana-Farber's leadership in the Cancer AI Alliance (CAIA), launched in October 2024 with MSK, Fred Hutchinson, and Johns Hopkins, aims to build a federated learning platform combining data from over one million patients with an aspirational goal of $1 billion in resources.

Mayo Clinic's AI for pancreatic cancer detection demonstrates the most advanced clinical application, achieving detection a **median 438 days earlier** than conventional methods and improving five-year survival rates from 13% overall to **44% when detected early**. The AI-PACED clinical trial launched to screen 10,000 patients, but Mayo estimates **3-5 years** for broad clinical rollout pending regulatory clearance and safety validation. Stanford Cancer Center developed **MUSK (Multimodal transformer with Unified maSKed modeling)**, trained on 50 million pathology images and 1 billion text tokens across 33 tumor types, and partnered with Microsoft to deploy agentic AI for tumor boards. Stanford appointed an Associate Director for Cancer AI and secured $8.9 million in ARPA-H funding for a multimodal oncology data lake.

MD Anderson Cancer Center established its Institute for Data Science in Oncology and AI Advisory Committee in 2025, pursuing partnerships with NVIDIA and Microsoft, but specific adoption rates remain undisclosed. Across all major centers, the pattern is consistent: sophisticated infrastructure development and governance frameworks coexist with limited direct clinical deployment for treatment decisions.

## Clinical validation studies show promise but critical evidence gaps persist

The strongest clinical evidence exists for **clinical trial matching**, where multiple converging studies demonstrate 60-95% accuracy. The PRISM study's OncoLLM, a 14-billion parameter model fine-tuned on 5,790 patients, achieved **68% question-level accuracy** for trial eligibility, matching qualified medical doctors' performance—the first study demonstrating LLM-physician equivalence. Remarkably, OncoLLM costs **$0.17 per patient-trial pair** compared to **$6.18 for GPT-4**, a 35-fold reduction. When reviewers examined OncoLLM's top-ranked trials that patients didn't actually enroll in, 9 of 10 were genuinely eligible, validating the model's recommendations. Clinical trial matching reduced manual review time from hours to **7.9-12.4 minutes per patient** in head and neck cancer studies, addressing a critical bottleneck in trial enrollment.

For treatment recommendation concordance with tumor boards, performance varies dramatically by cancer type and complexity. The **MEREDITH system** (Google Gemini Pro with retrieval-augmented generation) achieved **94.7% concordance** with molecular tumor board decisions on 10 fictional oncology cases, the highest reported rate. Real-world validation studies show breast cancer concordance ranging from **50-100%** depending on disease stage, with straightforward primary cancers achieving 96-100% concordance but metastatic stage IV tumors dropping to **50%**. An Ankara University study of 610 real patients across multiple cancer types found GPT-4 achieved a mean compatibility score of **3.59 out of 4** with excellent interrater reliability (Cronbach's alpha **0.950**), though 10.2% of cases fell below acceptable thresholds.

Cancer progression prediction from radiology reports represents another area of strong validation. Woollie achieved **0.97 AUROC** at MSK for predicting progression across five cancer types, with pancreatic cancer reaching **0.98 AUROC**. External validation at UCSF maintained **0.88 AUROC** overall with lung cancer achieving **0.95 AUROC**. This cross-institutional validation demonstrates generalizability critical for real-world implementation, though the performance drop from 0.97 to 0.88 highlights challenges in model transferability.

**The most critical evidence gap is the complete absence of randomized controlled trials evaluating LLM-CDSS with patient outcomes as endpoints.** Zero RCTs measure impact on overall survival, progression-free survival, quality of life, or time to treatment. The evidence base consists entirely of retrospective validation studies comparing LLM outputs to expert decisions—concordance does not equal improved outcomes. A first-in-kind RCT to evaluate LLM influence on treatment plans for gastrointestinal malignancies has been announced but not yet completed, with primary outcomes measuring influence on treatment planning rather than patient survival. No studies compare LLM-CDSS to traditional rule-based systems, representing another fundamental gap. A systematic review in Nature Digital Medicine analyzing 56 studies through May 2024 found an average overall accuracy of **76.2%**, but diagnostic accuracy was lower at **67.4%**, and studies rarely assessed safety or harm. The meta-analysis of 39 oncology studies found an overall **hallucination rate of 23%** (95% CI: 17-28%), with physician-oriented prompts showing significantly higher hallucination rates than patient-oriented prompts.

## Technical architectures mature while hallucination risks demand attention

Oncology-specific LLMs from 2023-2025 demonstrate sophisticated technical implementations balancing performance with computational efficiency. **Woollie** (Memorial Sloan Kettering, 2025) exemplifies this approach using Meta's Llama-1 architecture with variants from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters. The 7B model runs on a single GPU for clinical deployment while achieving performance comparable to the 33B model (both reaching **0.90 accuracy** for cancer progression) with **3x faster inference**. Woollie's three-stage stacked alignment strategy mitigates catastrophic forgetting by progressively fine-tuning from foundation alignment (reasoning and conversation) through medical domain alignment (PubMedQA, MedMCQA) to oncology specialization using 38,719 radiology impressions from 3,402 patients across five cancer types.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, particularly **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**, have become the dominant training approach, freezing base LLM parameters while adding trainable low-rank matrices. This reduces computational requirements and prevents catastrophic forgetting while enabling specialization. A radiation oncology study found LLaMA-3 8B with LoRA fine-tuning achieved a clinical benefit score of **3.4 out of 4** from physician evaluation, with ROUGE scores demonstrating superiority over the larger LLaMA-2 13B model. The shift toward prompt engineering is evident in study methodologies: 28% of 2022-2024 studies employed prompt engineering versus 0% in 2019-2021, with temperature settings of 0.01-0.2 for deterministic clinical classification.

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** has emerged as the critical architecture for reducing hallucinations and grounding LLM recommendations in evidence. The MEREDITH system integrates PubMed clinical studies, ClinicalTrials.gov, medication databases, and ASCO/ESMO/NCCN guidelines through targeted search strings specific to patient diagnosis and tumor profile. Its multi-query decomposition generates up to 12 subqueries per patient case, retrieves the top 40 passages, applies Cohere Rerank 3 for relevance scoring, and selects the top 10 passages for context. This chain-of-thought RAG approach with four consecutive prompts (identify research, identify trials, identify guidelines, prioritize recommendations) achieved **91.0% factually correct statements** and improved completeness from **30.3% for GPT-4 alone to 87.2%** with RAG integration, though citation accuracy reached only **75.5%**.

Multimodal integration represents the frontier of technical development. **MUSK** (Stanford, Nature 2025) employs unified masked modeling on 50 million pathology images paired with 1 billion text tokens, enabling processing of unpaired, unlabeled data at scale. This vision-language foundation model excelled across 23 benchmarks including melanoma relapse prediction and immunotherapy response prediction in lung and GI cancers. The **Threads model** (arXiv 2025) trained on 47,171 H&E tissue sections paired with genomic and transcriptomic profiles creates the largest paired dataset to date, capturing tissue molecular composition through slide-level analysis of any size. Threads outperformed all baselines across 54 oncology tasks with remarkable generalizability and label efficiency, particularly for predicting rare events.

The autonomous AI agent architecture published in Nature Cancer (2025) integrates GPT-4 with vision transformers, MedSAM medical image segmentation, and web search tools accessing OncoKB, PubMed, and Google. This system detects microsatellite instability and KRAS/BRAF mutations from histopathology with **87.5% tool accuracy** and **91% correct clinical conclusions**, improving from **30.3% to 87.2% accuracy** compared to GPT-4 alone. However, authors note regulatory restrictions prevent GPT-4 clinical use due to its cloud-based nature, highlighting the tension between technical capability and deployment reality.

Despite these advances, hallucination risks remain a fundamental safety concern. A clinical note generation study analyzing 12,999 clinician-annotated sentences found a **1.47% hallucination rate** and **3.45% omission rate**, with **44% of hallucinations classified as "major"** (could impact patient diagnosis or management if uncorrected). An adversarial hallucination study testing six leading LLMs with 300 physician-designed vignettes containing fabricated information showed models repeated or elaborated on planted errors in **up to 83% of cases**. Simple mitigation prompts halved the rate but didn't eliminate risk. Hallucinations occur most frequently in treatment plan sections (20% of total hallucinations)—the most critical area containing direct patient care instructions. The four hallucination categories identified were fabrication (43%), negation (30%), contextual mixing (17%), and causality speculation (10%), with negation being most concerning as it contradicts clinically relevant facts.

## Regulatory frameworks evolve but liability questions remain unresolved

The FDA released comprehensive draft guidance in January 2025 titled "Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions: Lifecycle Management and Marketing Submission Recommendations," representing the first comprehensive framework for AI-enabled medical devices across the total product lifecycle. This guidance mandates device descriptions explicitly stating AI use and how AI achieves intended use, risk assessment and management documentation, data management and quality verification, model development details, validation across relevant demographic groups, performance monitoring plans, cybersecurity controls, and user interface transparency. The December 2024 final guidance on "Predetermined Change Control Plans for AI/ML-Enabled Device Software Functions" addresses the adaptive nature of ML algorithms, requiring documentation of planned modifications, modification protocols including retraining methods, and impact assessments evaluating risk-benefit tradeoffs.

**Critically, bias mitigation has become a regulatory mandate.** FDA's June 2024 transparency guidance requires evaluation of performance across age, sex, race, and ethnicity, with evidence that devices benefit all relevant demographic groups similarly. The regulatory emphasis on explainability varies by device risk and clinical context, with high-risk oncology applications requiring detailed reasoning transparency appropriate for intended users. As of November 2025, over **1,016 FDA-authorized AI/ML-enabled medical devices** exist, but **zero are LLM-based CDSS for oncology treatment recommendations**. The 71 oncology-specific AI devices cleared through 2023 focus primarily on cancer radiology (54.9%), pathology (19.7%), and radiation oncology (8.5%), with 96.2% using the 510(k) premarket notification pathway requiring demonstration of substantial equivalence to a predicate device.

The regulatory pathway challenges for LLM-CDSS are substantial. The 510(k) pathway averages 82 FDA review days but requires a predicate device, which doesn't exist for novel LLM applications. The De Novo pathway for devices without predicates averages 174 review days and establishes new device classifications. Premarket Approval (PMA), the most stringent pathway for Class III devices, averages 181 review days and requires clinical safety and efficacy data—applicable if LLM-CDSS involves life-sustaining decisions. The traditional regulatory paradigm wasn't designed for adaptive AI/ML, and many algorithm changes may require premarket review, creating regulatory uncertainty.

**Liability concerns constitute a major adoption barrier with no clear resolution.** The medicolegal ecosystem involves multiple potentially liable parties: physicians (medical malpractice for clinical decisions), healthcare systems (vicarious and direct liability), AI developers/manufacturers (products liability), healthcare workers (professional negligence), and malpractice insurers (coverage decisions). Courts have been reluctant to excuse physician malpractice based on reliance on incorrect information, whether from medical literature, equipment errors, or pharmaceutical company warnings. An experimental study surveying 2,000 U.S. adults as potential jurors found physicians receiving standard care recommendations from AI can reduce liability risk by accepting advice, but when AI recommends nonstandard care, no similar shielding effect exists from rejecting advice. This asymmetry may incentivize confirmatory rather than transformative AI use.

Hospitals face vicarious liability for employed physicians using AI under the "respondeat superior" doctrine, where negligence of subordinates is attributed to supervisors. If an AI algorithm acts as a subordinate, the institution may be liable for its errors. Direct liability extends to purchasing inadequate AI systems, failure to properly implement or maintain tools, inadequate staff training, and system-level integration failures. Manufacturers face products liability for design defects (flawed algorithms), failure to warn (inadequate information about limitations and risks), and manufacturing defects (coding errors). The black box problem makes determining error causes difficult, rapid algorithm updates complicate version-specific issue tracking, and it remains unclear whether AI constitutes a "product" or "service" for liability purposes.

The most significant liability gap is the absence of established case law for AI medical devices, creating ambiguity that serves as a substantial barrier to adoption. Physicians express concern about legal responsibility for AI diagnostic errors when they cannot fully understand the algorithm's reasoning. The EU's IVDR requirements mandate developers design tools with explainable and understandable AI systems enabling healthcare professionals to make responsible decisions, but translating this principle into practice remains challenging given the black box nature of many advanced models.

## Algorithmic bias threatens to deepen cancer care disparities

Training data underrepresentation creates systematic disadvantages for minority populations. Most U.S. patient data used for AI training comes from only three states: California, Massachusetts, and New York. African Americans and older individuals face gaping underrepresentation in clinical trial data used for algorithm training. Dermatological AI models lacking sufficient skin color representation can exacerbate disparities in cancer diagnoses. The overall mortality rate is nearly **30% higher for non-Hispanic Black patients** compared to non-Hispanic white patients, and biased AI systems risk widening this gap.

Genomic data bias presents particularly concerning implications for precision oncology. **FOXA1 mutations** in prostate cancer occur significantly more frequently in Black men versus white men, while **TP53 mutations** are significantly lower in Black men. Black women with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer have worse prognosis than white counterparts. AI models trained on skewed genetic data may fail to capture diagnostic and prognostic differences across racial groups, potentially recommending inappropriate treatments based on population averages that don't reflect individual molecular profiles.

A case study revealed an AI algorithm using healthcare spending as a proxy for illness prioritized affluent white patients over sicker Black patients, demonstrating how seemingly neutral technical choices embed systemic bias. Breast cancer detection AI trained predominantly on Caucasian patients may struggle to detect cancer in patients with darker skin. False negative rates are disproportionately higher for underrepresented populations, leading to fewer follow-up scans and more undiagnosed cancers. Algorithms fail to account for transportation barriers (patients in rural Mississippi lacking reliable transit access), limited internet access in underserved communities, and different cultural presentations and disease incidence rates not captured in training data.

**Developer diversity compounds these problems.** Only 5% of active physicians in 2018 identified as Black and about 6% as Hispanic or Latinx, with developer diversity even lower than physician diversity. This lack of domain expertise and lived experience contributes to bias propagation. Three mitigation strategies have emerged: pre-processing approaches ensure representative datasets and address class imbalance through data augmentation; in-processing approaches use mathematical incentives for balanced predictions and fairness constraints in optimization; post-processing approaches adjust decision thresholds for different demographic groups and implement continuous performance monitoring. However, research gaps persist, particularly insufficient discussion of ethical challenges in low- and middle-income countries and limited prospective validation across diverse populations.

A systematic analysis found that **76.5% of oncologists feel responsible for protecting patients from biased AI, but only 27.9% feel confident they can identify bias**—a dangerous gap between responsibility and capability. The FDA now mandates evaluation across all relevant demographic groups, but ensuring representativeness in datasets and collecting evidence that devices benefit all groups similarly remain substantial technical and logistical challenges. One quantitative study found no significant performance differences by demographics in the Woollie model, but sample sizes were limited and may not capture rare subgroup disparities.

## Oncologist perspectives reveal cautious acceptance with critical capability gaps

The most comprehensive U.S. survey of 201 oncologists (Dana-Farber/Blood 2023) revealed **95.6% believe AI can help in diagnosing** cancer, **89.2% in treating**, and 60.4% in managing side effects. However, this optimism coexists with substantial concerns. **67.1% require AI prognostic models to be explainable** and 62.1% require clinical decision models to be explainable, yet current LLMs often function as black boxes. **81.4% believe patients should consent to AI use** in treatment decisions, but consent practices remain inconsistent across institutions. When asked about responsibility for medico-legal problems, **90.5% believe AI developers should be responsible** while 47.8% believe physicians should also bear responsibility—highlighting confusion about liability allocation.

The most revealing finding concerns the **confidence-responsibility gap in bias detection**: 76.5% of oncologists feel responsible for protecting patients from biased AI, yet only 27.9% feel confident they can spot bias. This disconnect creates a dangerous scenario where physicians may provide false assurance of bias-free recommendations. When AI and oncologist recommendations disagree, **37.3% would present both options and let the patient decide**, effectively shifting the decision burden to patients who may lack technical literacy to evaluate AI recommendations. Notably, **56.1% of academic hospital physicians** would recommend the AI-generated regimen versus **69.0% of non-academic physicians** sticking with their original plan (OR 2.85, p=0.007), suggesting practice variation based on institutional setting that could exacerbate healthcare disparities.

A Chinese oncologist survey of 228 participants found only **12.8% had used ChatGPT**, with moderate understanding of AI concepts (mean 3.39/5) and **52.19% expressing trust** in AI technology. Trust and acceptance were significantly higher among younger physicians (approximately 30 years old, p=0.004 for trust, p=0.009 for acceptance) and those with master's or doctorate degrees versus bachelor's degrees (p=0.032). **67.1% were willing to learn about AI** and 67.98% perceived a need for oncologist AI training, but many don't know where to find training resources. A German mixed methods study found that attitudes were shaped more by experience and engagement than demographics, with concerns decreasing as familiarity increased through professional use.

**Training requirements remain largely unmet.** Essential components include foundational AI literacy (capabilities, limitations, bias recognition), clinical application training (when to trust versus question AI outputs, verification protocols), bias detection and mitigation (critical given the 76.5% to 27.9% responsibility-capability gap), ethical and legal training (patient consent, liability, privacy), and technical troubleshooting (recognizing hallucinations, understanding confidence levels). Hands-on practice proves most effective for building confidence, yet systematic training programs remain rare. Mayo Clinic provides FAST team support, MSK maintains a dedicated 11-person AI/ML Solutions Engineering team, and leading centers have appointed Chief Digital Officers, but these resources remain concentrated at elite institutions.

Patient attitudes add complexity to implementation decisions. A study of 2,675 diverse U.S. patients revealed "AI aversion"—**decreased trust when AI involvement is mentioned**, with trust highest when AI is explicitly absent. Patients prefer human-only or AI-assisted (not AI-autonomous) care, expressing concerns about depersonalization, bias, and data security. This creates a paradox: mentioning AI involvement (required for informed consent per 81.4% of oncologists) decreases patient trust, yet concealing AI use raises ethical concerns. Physician recommendations prove critical for patient acceptance, placing additional pressure on oncologists to understand and endorse AI tools they may not fully trust themselves.

## Foundation models and federated learning chart the technological future

The oncology AI landscape is undergoing a paradigm shift from specialized single-task models to comprehensive **multimodal foundation models** capable of integrating diverse data types. **MUSK** (Stanford, Nature January 2025) represents this evolution, pretrained on 50 million pathology images from 11,577 patients plus 1 billion pathology text tokens, then further trained on 1 million pathology image-text pairs. This unified masked modeling approach allows processing unpaired, unlabeled data at scale, enabling the model to excel across 23 benchmarks including melanoma relapse prediction, pan-cancer prognosis, and immunotherapy response prediction in lung and gastrointestinal cancers. The key innovation is combining pathology images with clinical reports for precision cancer therapy, effectively bridging visual and textual medical information.

**Threads** (arXiv preprint January 2025) takes molecular integration further, training on 47,171 H&E tissue sections paired with genomic and transcriptomic profiles—the largest paired dataset to date. This slide-level foundation model for whole-slide images of any size captures tissue underlying molecular composition through multimodal learning. Benchmarked on 54 oncology tasks including subtyping, grading, mutation prediction, IHC status, treatment response, and survival, Threads outperformed all baselines with remarkable generalizability and label efficiency, particularly excelling at predicting rare events. Model weights will be made publicly available, potentially accelerating field-wide progress.

**Med-PaLM 2** (Google/DeepMind, Nature Medicine January 2025) achieved **85-86.5% accuracy on USMLE-style questions** (MedQA dataset), representing a 19% improvement over Med-PaLM 1. Physicians preferred Med-PaLM 2 answers over physician answers on 8 of 9 clinical axes, marking the first time an LLM exceeded human expert preferences. The successor MedLM family, launched December 2023 and available via Google Cloud Vertex AI, offers two model sizes (larger for complex tasks, medium for fine-tuning). A Gemini-fine-tuned version reached **91.1% on USMLE benchmarks** with multimodal capabilities. Med-PaLM M, the multimodal variant, synthesizes information from chest X-rays, mammograms, and multiple imaging modalities, though Google acknowledges "serious gaps when it comes to answering medical queries" and emphasizes the need for rigorous quality assessment before diagnostic or treatment use.

**Federated learning** has emerged as the solution for privacy-preserving multi-institutional collaboration. The **DigiONE (Digital Oncology Network for Europe)** six-center technology pilot by Digital Institute for Cancer Outcomes Research established a privacy-preserving network with cancer data in OMOP common data model format (Nature Medicine March 2024). This core cancer dataset with molecular data, treatment, and outcomes enables protocolized research across federated nodes, transforming the speed and richness of European real-world evidence. Studies demonstrate that federated learning often matches or exceeds centralized machine learning performance—15 of 25 studies in a systematic review showed equal or superior results—while enhancing generalizability across diverse populations and maintaining institutional data governance.

Privacy-preserving technologies enable federated approaches. **Homomorphic encryption** allows computation on encrypted data, with the FAMHE system demonstrating truly private federated evaluation in oncology, though computational overhead limits use to non-time-sensitive applications. **Differential privacy** injects statistical noise into model updates to prevent data leakage, with a Nature study (April 2025) achieving **96.1% accuracy** for breast cancer detection with a privacy budget of ε=1.9. Secure aggregation prevents exposure of individual institution gradients by performing model averaging in trusted, inaccessible environments. Demonstrated applications include lung cancer screening, prostate cancer MRI interpretation, brain tumor segmentation, and breast cancer pathology classification.

**Agentic AI systems** represent the evolution toward autonomous clinical workflows. GE HealthCare's example architecture employs a coordinating agent aggregating EMR data, specialist agents for radiology, pathology, biochemical, and genomic analysis, and a recommendation generator applying clinical decision support algorithms. Stanford's partnership with Microsoft deploys Healthcare Agent Orchestrator in Azure AI Foundry to address physician overload (1.5-2.5 hours per patient for tumor board data review) through multimodal data workflow coordination. Aidoc's "always-on" radiologist assistant performs autonomous triage of urgent findings like pulmonary embolism and intracranial hemorrhage, with priority flagging for time-sensitive cases integrated seamlessly with PACS.

Current agentic applications focus on documentation (ambient listening technology from athenahealth and Augmedix), administrative tasks (up to 40% IT workload reduction), clinical trial recruitment, and appointment optimization. However, ASCO 2024 and HIMSS 2025 conferences highlighted critical cautions: variable concordance rates for complex cases (Stage IV management: 50% agreement in some studies), risk of biased decision-making without proper oversight, governance lagging behind technology advancement, and current regulations designed for ML rather than autonomous agents. Expert recommendations emphasize starting with administrative tasks before clinical decisions, establishing clear accountability frameworks, and staged implementation with defined human checkpoints.

## Eighteen adoption barriers span technical to organizational domains

**Technical barriers** begin with data quality and standardization challenges. The lack of structured, standardized cancer-related health data combines with variability in EHR data collection within systems and limited interoperability across healthcare systems globally. Imaging systems acquire annotations in formats that don't permit AI reuse, and lack of Common Oncology Data Elements implementation hinders progress. Algorithm performance suffers from limited reproducibility when transferred across healthcare systems and populations, with the Epic Sepsis Model failing when applied outside its development institution exemplifying dataset shift problems. The black box effect poses a tremendous barrier in consequential healthcare decisions, with deep learning models lacking transparency in decision-making making it difficult for clinicians to determine biological or clinical rationale.

**Workflow and usability barriers** emerge from clinics not set up for AI integration, with busy and complicated workflows where adding AI increases clerical and cognitive burden for providers. The divide between those implementing and those using AI models creates adoption friction. Facility-to-facility interoperability is lacking, intra-facility system compatibility presents problems, and point-of-care integration proves difficult. New datastreams from mobile health devices and wearables present unique integration challenges. AI-based CDSS require interactive and explanatory interfaces, yet dynamic and adaptive aspects often aren't apparent to end users, and visualizations of AI recommendations may inappropriately affect decision-making.

**Validation and evidence barriers** reveal the gap between algorithm development and clinical impact. Despite thousands of published algorithm performance studies, only 9 prospective trials and 2 randomized trials of deep learning in medical imaging existed as of 2020. Few studies compare AI/ML interventions with standard of care on patient outcomes, creating uncertainty about impact on outcomes and cost-effectiveness. Inconsistent reporting of source code and training conditions, insufficient documentation of model development, and limited transparency about training data sources make reproducing results across institutions difficult.

**Economic and reimbursement barriers** stem from the vast majority of commercially-available AI devices not currently being reimbursed, making it challenging to assess true extent of clinical integration. Traditional insurance claims data cannot measure AI device use, creating uncertainty about return on investment. Healthcare value (outcome achieved per dollar spent) hasn't been rigorously assessed, leaving unclear whether AI improves resource allocation and access to quality care. High implementation costs for data infrastructure, training, and maintenance combine with uncertain cost-effectiveness to deter adoption.

**Regulatory and legal barriers** include rapid AI evolution outpacing regulatory guidance, lack of specific approval systems in some jurisdictions, and delays in approval hindering progress. The black box problem prevents regulators from understanding and trusting AI, while absence of established case law for AI medical devices creates uncertainty. Ambiguous malpractice liability policies and physician concern about legal responsibility for AI errors may incentivize confirmatory rather than transformative use, discouraging proactive adoption.

**Ethical and trust barriers** require physician buy-in and engagement that proves challenging. Lack of engagement may produce clinical problems, and building trust requires transparency about model development and data sources that black box models cannot provide. Inherent bias in training datasets means AI may perpetuate and exacerbate existing healthcare disparities. Protecting patient privacy when analyzing sensitive health data required for AI efficacy raises concerns, with large datasets creating cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Data breaches could compromise patient information while compliance with HIPAA and international data protection regulations adds complexity.

**Human factors barriers** include physician resistance from ignorance or reluctance to understand AI, fear of being replaced rather than augmented, concern about deskilling and loss of autonomy, and generational differences in technology acceptance. Over-reliance risk exists where clinicians may blindly trust AI recommendations, with potential for diminishing critical thinking skills over time. Oncology decisions require nuanced judgment beyond standardized guidelines, making maintaining the human element in decision-making crucial. Inadequate AI literacy among healthcare professionals, need for domain-specific training on capabilities and limitations, insufficient understanding of when to trust versus override AI, and lack of formal AI curricula in medical education all impede effective implementation.

**Organizational barriers** emerge from inherent organizational complexity of cancer care delivery, the need to interpret and synthesize vast amounts of data from different sources, heterogeneity of cancer making standardization difficult, and multidisciplinary care coordination challenges. Healthcare IT teams are overburdened with AI system maintenance requiring continuous monitoring, updating, and troubleshooting. Hardware obsolescence requires frequent replacement, creating substantial electronic waste and environmental impact. Resistance to changing established workflows, need for organizational culture shift, insufficient leadership support, lack of clear implementation pathways, and inadequate stakeholder involvement in development all slow adoption.

## Lessons learned from implementation reveal success factors

The Memorial Sloan Kettering iLEAP model exemplifies comprehensive AI governance with three distinct pathways (research, in-house build, acquired/purchased), each with tailored oversight. Models undergo risk-based assessment scored as Low/Medium/High, determining oversight intensity. Gate reviews at five stages (G1-G5) ensure thorough evaluation before production deployment, with Express Path options for well-documented low-risk models enabling 2-week turnaround versus months for standard review. The Model Information Sheet standardizes documentation, while the Model Registry enables centralized tracking of all AI models and their status. However, even this sophisticated framework required intensive resources: MSK's 63% increase in AI project intake from 2024 to 2025 (from 5 vendor model proposals to 15) demonstrates scaling challenges.

**Phased deployment starting with non-clinical applications** proves essential. Dana-Farber explicitly excluded clinical care from GPT4DFCI use, allowing 2,000+ users to gain experience with AI capabilities and limitations in lower-stakes administrative and research contexts. Silent evaluation mode tests algorithms in the background before clinical integration, limited pilots enable small-scale testing before enterprise rollout, and iterative refinement incorporates continuous feedback loops. Northwestern Medicine's integration of Tempus "David" AI co-pilot into their EHR as the first health system to deploy a generative AI clinical co-pilot demonstrates successful workflow embedding, though specific adoption metrics remain undisclosed.

**Human-in-the-loop requirements** are non-negotiable across all successful implementations. AI serves as decision support, not decision maker. Expert review by molecular tumor boards assesses AI-generated treatment options, dual review systems have both AI and human experts evaluate cases, and clinicians maintain ultimate decision authority with override capabilities. MSK's risk assessment explicitly includes human oversight as a key mitigation factor, with models scored higher risk without physician supervision. Mayo Clinic's AI-PACED trial for pancreatic cancer detection, while demonstrating earlier detection by 438 days, requires full clinical trial validation before deployment, exemplifying responsible innovation pacing.

**Domain-specific training outperforms general-purpose approaches.** Woollie, trained specifically on oncology radiology reports, achieved 0.97 AUROC for cancer progression versus general-purpose LLMs. MEREDITH's integration of oncology-specific guidelines and trial databases via RAG improved concordance from 77.1% to 94.7%. Threads' molecular-histology pairing captures information general vision models miss. The technical consensus emerging from 2023-2025 research favors 7B-13B parameter models for general oncology (balancing performance and efficiency), 33B+ models for complex reasoning, and prioritization of 7B models for single-GPU clinical deployment. Fine-tuning should employ LoRA/PEFT methods to preserve base knowledge, and RAG implementation should combine semantic search with keyword matching, employ reranking for relevance, utilize metadata for filtering, and maintain citation tracking for clinical trust.

Validation strategies must include cross-institutional testing as mandatory before deployment, human expert review by 4+ independent reviewers with blinding, prioritization of real-world data over synthetic benchmarks, and continuous post-deployment performance tracking. Drift management requires multi-dimensional monitoring (statistical, performance, semantic), healthcare-specific tracking of population shifts and guideline updates, automated alerts with threshold-based triggers, and risk-based retraining schedules (daily for high-stakes applications, periodic for others). The technical sophistication demonstrated in 2023-2025 implementations is remarkable, but the gap between technical capability and clinical deployment reveals that technology alone is insufficient—governance, training, validation, and trust-building are equally critical.

## Conclusions: navigating the gap between technological promise and clinical reality

The comprehensive research from 2023-2025 reveals a field characterized by technical maturity coexisting with clinical immaturity. **Sophisticated architectures exist**: 7B-65B parameter models with parameter-efficient fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation reducing hallucinations, multimodal foundation models integrating imaging with genomics, and federated learning enabling privacy-preserving collaboration. **Validation studies show promise**: 60-95% tumor board concordance, 68% clinical trial matching accuracy matching physicians, 0.88-0.97 AUROC for cancer progression prediction with cross-institutional validation, and 91% correct clinical conclusions for multimodal agents with tool access.

Yet **critical gaps persist** that prevent widespread clinical deployment. **Zero FDA-approved LLM-CDSS** exist for oncology treatment recommendations. **Zero RCTs demonstrate patient outcome improvements** from LLM-CDSS use. **Safety concerns remain unresolved**: 1.47-23% hallucination rates with 44% classified as major errors, adversarial testing showing up to 83% error repetition from fabricated inputs, and treatment plan sections most vulnerable to dangerous hallucinations. **Regulatory pathways are evolving** but unclear, with comprehensive 2025 FDA guidance establishing requirements yet lacking specific approval precedents. **Liability questions are unresolved** across multiple stakeholders, with absence of case law creating adoption hesitancy and potential for defensive rather than transformative use.

**Algorithmic bias threatens to exacerbate existing disparities**, with training data underrepresenting African Americans and other minorities, genomic data skewed toward European ancestry populations, and 30% higher mortality rates for non-Hispanic Black patients potentially widening if AI systems encode existing inequities. **The oncologist confidence-capability gap** is dangerous: 76.5% feel responsible for protecting patients from bias, only 27.9% feel confident identifying it, creating false assurance scenarios. **Adoption barriers span 18 categories** from technical data quality issues through organizational resistance, with the vast majority of commercially available AI devices unreimbursed and uncertain return on investment.

**What works**: Memorial Sloan Kettering's iLEAP governance model with risk-stratified pathways and Express Pass options for low-risk applications; Dana-Farber's phased deployment starting with non-clinical use while building governance and user literacy; Mayo Clinic's rigorous clinical trial validation (AI-PACED) before deployment despite earlier detection capability; Stanford's domain-specific foundation model development (MUSK) with public release plans; federated learning consortia (DigiONE, CAIA) enabling multi-institutional collaboration while preserving privacy; RAG implementations reducing hallucinations through evidence grounding; and human-in-the-loop requirements maintaining physician decision authority.

**What's needed urgently**: Randomized controlled trials with patient outcome endpoints (survival, quality of life, time to treatment) rather than concordance metrics; prospective real-world implementation studies with comprehensive safety surveillance; standardized reporting frameworks for AI development and validation following emerging TRIPOD-AI guidelines; diverse, representative training datasets addressing health equity with prospective validation across populations; clear legal frameworks allocating liability appropriately across developers, institutions, and clinicians; seamless workflow integration reducing rather than increasing clinician burden; payment models supporting AI value in healthcare rather than fee-for-service misalignment; and systematic AI literacy training for oncologists covering capabilities, limitations, bias detection, and when to trust versus override recommendations.

**The field stands at a pivotal juncture** where technological capability has outpaced regulatory frameworks, clinical trust, training infrastructure, and evidence of patient benefit. The next 3-5 years will determine whether LLM-based CDSS achieve meaningful impact on cancer outcomes or remain sophisticated tools confined to research settings and administrative tasks. Success requires coordinated action across developers prioritizing safety and explainability over performance metrics alone, regulators establishing clear pathways that balance innovation with patient protection, healthcare systems investing in governance infrastructure and training programs, professional societies developing standards and best practices, payers creating reimbursement models that reward value not volume, and researchers conducting the rigorous clinical trials that remain conspicuously absent from the evidence base. The promise is extraordinary—earlier cancer detection, precision-matched treatments, reduced disparities, and augmented clinical decision-making. The reality as of 2025 is that promise remains largely unrealized, with the gap between capability and deployment revealing that technology, however sophisticated, is only one component of the transformation required to realize AI's potential in cancer care.
